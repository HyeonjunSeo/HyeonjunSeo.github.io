SYDE572 — Assignment 2

Polynomial regression and Gaussian basis function regression

Part 1: Polynomial Regression

(a) Training and testing RMS versus polynomial degree

Comment: Training RMS decreases as polynomial degree increases, from 2.0372 for degree 1 to 0.8815 for degree 7. In contrast, testing RMS increases dramatically, from 36.4449 for degree 1 to approximately 2.11 × 10²⁷ for degree 7. This indicates severe overfitting and poor generalization at higher degrees. The extremely large testing errors also suggest numerical instability caused by raising unnormalized feature values to high powers.

(b) Linear regression weights

The fitted degree-1 model produced the following weights, with the bias first:

[22.3090, -1.337974, 0.122993, -0.531065, approximately 0, 1.002651, 4.195634, -2.166275, 0.010715, -0.193778, -0.348631, 0.261264, 0.181073, 0.148761]

  • Bias weight: 22.3090
  • Weight on the 6th feature (RM): 4.1956
  • Weight on the 8th feature (DIS): 0.010715

Are the weights reasonable? The weight for RM (average number of rooms per dwelling) is positive (w₅ = 4.1956), which is reasonable because houses with more rooms would generally be expected to have higher median values, holding other features constant.

The weight for DIS (weighted distance to five Boston employment centres) is relatively close to zero (w₇ = 0.010715), suggesting a small linear contribution in this fitted model. However, a small coefficient does not necessarily mean that distance is unimportant. The coefficient's magnitude depends on the feature's scale, and its effect may overlap with other features or involve nonlinear relationships.

(d) Selecting the regularization constant

Comment: The lowest average validation RMS was 9.9839, at λ = 10. Therefore, λ = 10 is selected for the degree-8 polynomial model based on these cross-validation results. The unregularized model produced a much larger average validation RMS of 177529.8719. Regularization substantially reduces the validation error by controlling the magnitude of the polynomial coefficients and improving generalization.

Part 2: Gaussian Basis Function Regression

(e) Training and testing RMS versus number of basis functions

Comment: Training RMS decreases consistently from 3.5371 with 5 basis functions to 0.4234 with 95 basis functions, indicating that increasing the number of basis functions allows the model to fit the training data more closely.

Testing RMS initially decreases from 13.5540 at K = 5 to 7.8863 at K = 25. The lowest observed testing RMS is 7.9354 at K = 35?

As K increases further, testing RMS fluctuates and eventually rises sharply, reaching 113.0822 at K = 85 and 217.4293 at K = 95. This indicates that adding more basis functions does not necessarily improve generalization. Although the training error continues to decrease, the model becomes increasingly sensitive to the training data, potentially due to overfitting and numerical ill-conditioning. Among the tested configurations, K = 25 provides the lowest testing RMS of 7.8863.

(f) Selecting the regularization constant

Comment: The lowest average validation RMS was 3.8081, at λ = 0.1. Therefore, cross-validation selects λ = 0.1 for the Gaussian basis regression model. The validation RMS decreases slightly from 3.9035 without regularization to 3.8081 at λ = 0.1. Larger regularization constants increase the validation error, suggesting that excessive penalization restricts the model too strongly. A small amount of regularization provides the best balance between fitting the training data and generalizing to unseen data among the tested values.
